Geometric measure theory studies fractal measures and dimensions. We outline here a standard method for building outer meaures from premeasures, which guides the standard construction of Hausdorff measures and dimension. These notes are biased to put a spotlight on Hausdorff dimension as a principal quantity of fractal geometry. This is despite the established importance of other fractal measures/dimensions and their established connections with AIT.

## Measures

We begin by reviewing premeasures and their associated outer measures. For more details, refer to {cite:p}`Rei08` and {cite:p}`Rog70`.

:::{prf:definition}
:label: definition:premeasure
Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a collection of subsets of a set $\Omega$ containing $\emptyset$. Then a **premeasure** on $\mathcal{C}$ is a function $\rho : \mathcal{C} \to [0, +\infty]$ and satisfies $\rho(\emptyset) = 0$.
:::

In the setting of Cantor space, it is customary to consider a premeasure on $2^\omega$ to be any non-negative mapping defined on the {term}`cylinder sets <Cylinder set>` $\rho : \mathcal{B} \to [0, +\infty]$. Of course, such maps are in one-to-one correspondence with maps defined on the finite binary strings $\rho: 2^{<\omega} \to [0, +\infty]$. We may freely interchange between these two types by identifying any $\sigma \in 2^{<\omega}$ with its cylinder set $[\sigma] \in \mathcal{B}$.

In metric spaces, there is a standard, general method (introduced as “Method II” in {cite:t}`Rog70`) to induce an outer measure from a given premeasure.

:::{prf:theorem} Theorem 15 of {cite:t}`Rog70`
Let $\rho$ be a premeasure on a collection $\mathcal{C}$ of subsets of a metric space $(\Omega, d)$. Define for each $X \subseteq \Omega$ and $\delta > 0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{H}^\rho_\delta(X) &:= \inf_{(C_i)_{i \in \omega} \subseteq \mathcal{C}} \set{\sum_{i} \rho(C_i)}{\diam_d(C_i) \leq \delta, \bigcup_i C_i \supseteq X}, \quad \text{and} \quad \mathcal{H}^\rho(X) := \sup_{\delta > 0} \mathcal{H}^\rho_\delta(X).
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, $\mathcal{H}^\rho$ is an outer measure on $\Omega$.
:::

We call $\mathcal{H}^\rho$ the **Method II outer measure associated to the premeasure $\rho$**. And for each $\delta > 0$, we also call $\mathcal{H}^\rho_\delta$ the **$\rho$-dimensional $\delta$-content** measure.

In {cite:t}`Rog70`, C. Rogers also defines an alternative “Method I” which differs from Method II in that it does not enforce any upper bound on the diameter of the cover elements used in the infimum:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{H}^\rho_{\operatorname{I}}(X) &:= \inf_{(C_i)_{i \in \omega} \subseteq \mathcal{C}} \set{\sum_{i} \rho(C_i)}{\bigcup_i C_i \supseteq X}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This outer measure is also sometimes denoted $\mathcal{H}^\rho_\infty$. While this method also produces an outer measure from a premeasure $\rho$ which agrees on null sets with $\mathcal{H}^\rho$, Rogers points out that only the results of Method II can be guaranteed to be additive on sets which are separated by positive distance {cite:p}`Rog70`. We proceed with Method II as our preferred way to produce outer measures from premeasures.

Cantor space: $2^\omega$, is a metric space when imbued with the standard metric,

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(x, y) := \begin{cases}
2^{-N} & \text{if $x \neq y$ and $N = \min\set{n \in \omega}{x(n) \neq y(n)}$},\\
0 & \text{if $x = y$}.
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$

Note that this metric is compatible with the product topology generated by the cylinder sets in $\mathcal{B}$.

## Hausdorff Measures

An important class of outer measures on any metric space $(\Omega, d)$ are the **Hausdorff outer measures**, which are those Method II outer measures induced from premeasures whose values depend only on the diameter of the set. First, declare a **dimension function** (a.k.a., **gauge**) to be any non-negative, non-decreasing, continuous-on-the-right function $h$ defined on all non-negative real numbers and satisfying $h(t) = 0 \iff t = 0$. One may associate to any dimension function $h$ its **Hausdorff premeasure** defined on all $X \subseteq \Omega$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho_h(X) := (h \circ \diam_d)(X).
\end{aligned}
$$

Denote by $\mathcal{H}^h := \mathcal{H}^{\rho_h}$ the Hausdorff outer measure associated to $h$.

In Cantor space, $\rho_h(\sigma) = h\p{2^{- \operatorname{len}(\sigma)}}$ for any $\sigma \in 2^{<\omega}$. So, any Hausdorff premeasure on $2^\omega$ is **length-invariant**, in that $\rho_h(\sigma)$ only depends on the length of $\sigma$. 

Later, we will briefly focus on **convex** Hausdorff premeasures $\rho$ over Cantor space, which satisfy:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho(\sigma) \leq \rho(\sigma^\smallfrown 0) + \rho(\sigma^\smallfrown 1),
\end{aligned}
$$

for all $\sigma \in 2^{<\omega}$. It is easy to see that all length-invariant, convex Hausdorff premeasures over $2^\omega$ are also **strongly convex**, meaning for all $\sigma \in 2^{<\omega}$ and $i \in \seq{0, 1}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho(\sigma ^\smallfrown i) \geq \frac{\rho(\sigma)}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Essential to fractal geometry is the family of **$s$-dimensional Hausdorff premeasures** $\rho_s := \rho_{h_s}$, where $h_s : t \mapsto t^s$ is the dimension function associated to $s \geq 0$. If $\delta > 0$, let $\mathcal{H}^s_{\delta} := \mathcal{H}^{\rho_s}_\delta$ denote the **$s$-dimensional Hausdorff $\delta$-content**, and $\mathcal{H}^s := \mathcal{H}^{\rho_s}$ the **$s$-dimensional Hausdorff outer measure**.

For any outer measure $\mu$ on a metric space $(\Omega, d)$, we say a set $X \subseteq \Omega$ is **$\mu$-null** if $\mu(X) = 0$. Any $\mathcal{H}^s$-null $X$ is also called **$s$-null**. And, if $\mu$ and $\nu$ are outer measures on $\Omega$, then $\nu$ is said to be **absolutely continuous** with respect to $\mu$, written $\nu \ll \mu$, if every $\mu$-null set is also $\nu$-null. A set $X \subseteq \Omega$ is **$\mu$-conull** if $\Omega \setminus X$ is $\mu$-null. More generally, a subset $Y \subseteq X$ is **$\mu$-conull in $X$** if $X \setminus Y$ is $\mu$-null. 

Suppose $\mathcal{H}^s(X) < \infty$ for some $s \geq 0$. This implies that $X$ is $s_+$-null for any $s_+ > s$ too. Alternatively, if $\mathcal{H}^s(X) > 0$ for some $s \geq 0$, then it must be that $\mathcal{H}^{s_-}(X) = \infty$ for all $0 \leq s_- < s$. So, the shape of the function $s \mapsto \mathcal{H}^s(X)$ is either constant with value $0$ or $\infty$, or a step from $\infty$ to $0$ (where, at the critical value of $s$ where the step occurs, $\mathcal{H}^s(X)$ could take on any value in $[0, \infty]$). This motivates the following definition for the **Hausdorff dimension** of a set $X$ {cite:p}`Hau19; Fal90`:

```{math}
:label: equation:definition_of_Hausdorff_dimension
\begin{aligned}
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(X) := \inf\set{s \geq 0}{\text{$X$ is $s$-null}},
\end{aligned}
```

or $+\infty$ if the infimum is taken over the empty set.

Consider now how some sets fall between the cracks of the power functions $h_s$: a set of Hausdorff dimension $s$ could have $\mathcal{H}^s(X) \in \seq{0, +\infty}$ (i.e., be $s$-null or have infinite $\mathcal{H}^s$-measure). In either case, no power function $h_{s'}$ assigns $X$ a positive, finite measure. Still, it is possible that some dimension function does: call a dimension function $h$ an **exact gauge** for $X$ if $0 < \mathcal{H}^h(X) < +\infty$. 

And exact gauges still relate back to Hausdorff dimension through their relationship with power laws:

:::{prf:proposition}
:label: proposition:exact_gauge_orders
If $h$ is an exact gauge for $X$, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\liminf_{t \to 0^+} \frac{\log h(t)}{\log t} \leq \dim_{\operatorname{H}}(X) \leq \limsup_{t \to 0^+} \frac{\log h(t)}{\log t}.
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

:::{prf:proof}
Let $s := \dim_{\operatorname{H}}(X)$. 

First, if $0 < u$ is less than the limit inferior, then $h(t) \leq t^u$ for all sufficiently small $t > 0$. So $\mathcal{H}^h_\delta(X) \leq \mathcal{H}^u_\delta(X)$ for all sufficiently small $\delta > 0$, and $0 < \mathcal{H}^h(X) \leq \mathcal{H}^u(X)$. Hence $u \leq s$. 

Now, if $v$ exceeds the limit superior, then $h(t) \geq t^v$ for all sufficiently small $t > 0$, so $\mathcal{H}^v(X) \leq \mathcal{H}^h(X) < \infty$. Hence $s \leq v$.
:::

## Packing Dimension

Over Euclidean space, a second standard fractal dimension is built from the covering numbers:

:::{prf:definition}
:label: definition:delta_ball_covering_number
For any bounded set $X \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ and $\delta > 0$, define the **$\delta$-ball covering number** of $X$ to be:

$$
\begin{aligned}
N(X, \delta) := \min \set{\abs{Y}}{\text{$Y$ is a set of $\delta$-balls covering $X$}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

:::{prf:definition}
:label: definition:upper_box_counting_dimension
The **upper box-counting dimension** of a non-empty bounded set $X \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\overline{\dim}_{\operatorname{B}}(X) := \limsup_{\delta \to 0^+} \frac{\log N(X, \delta)}{-\log \delta},
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

:::{prf:definition}
:label: definition:packing_dimension
The **packing dimension** of any $X \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dim_{\operatorname{P}}(X) := \inf \set{\sup_{i \in \omega} \overline{\dim}_{\operatorname{B}}(X_i)}{\text{each $X_i$ is nonempty, bounded and } X \subseteq \textstyle\bigcup_{i \in \omega} X_i}.
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

While {cite:t}`Tri82` originally introduced packing dimension through packing measures, the formula above is its equivalent characterization as the modified upper box-counting dimension {cite:p}`Fal90`. We always have that $\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(X) \leq \dim_{\operatorname{P}}(X)$, and $\dim_{\operatorname{P}}(X) \leq \overline{\dim}_{\operatorname{B}}(X)$ whenever $X$ is bounded. Following {cite:t}`FS24`, a set $X \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ is said to be **weakly regular** if $\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(X) = \dim_{\operatorname{P}}(X)$.

## Hausdorff Dimension of Measures

An outer measure inherits Hausdorff dimension from the sets that carry its mass.

:::{prf:definition}
:label: definition:Hausdorff_dimension_of_a_measure
Let $\mu$ be an outer measure on $\Omega$ with $\mu(\Omega) > 0$. The **lower** and **upper Hausdorff dimensions** of $\mu$ are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\underline{\dim}_{\operatorname{H}}(\mu) &:= \inf \set{\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(E)}{\text{$E$ is not $\mu$-null}}, \quad \text{and}\\
\overline{\dim}_{\operatorname{H}}(\mu) &:= \inf \set{\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(E)}{\text{$E$ is $\mu$-conull}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

Intuitively, the lower Hausdorff dimension is small when at least *some* of its mass sits on a set of small dimension; whereas the upper Hausdorff dimension is small when *all* of its mass does. These definitions are usually stated for finite Borel measures over $\mathbb{R}^m$ {cite:p}`Fal97`.

Notice that every $\mu$-conull set $E$ has $\mu(E) = \mu(\Omega) > 0$, so we always have $\underline{\dim}_{\operatorname{H}}(\mu) \leq \overline{\dim}_{\operatorname{H}}(\mu)$.

For an outer measure $\mu$ on $\Omega$ and a set $X \subseteq \Omega$, let the **restriction** of $\mu$ to $X$ be the outer measure $\mu \llcorner X : E \mapsto \mu(E \cap X)$. We can also relate the dimension of $\mu \llcorner X$ back to that of $X$. Fix $X \subseteq \Omega$ with $\mu(X) > 0$. Notice that a set $E$ is $(\mu \llcorner X)$-conull exactly when $E \cap X$ is $\mu$-conull in $X$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
\overline{\dim}_{\operatorname{H}}(\mu \llcorner X) = \inf \set{\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(Y)}{\text{$Y \subseteq X$ is $\mu$-conull in $X$}} \leq \dim_{\operatorname{H}}(X).
\end{aligned}
$$

Equality here holds exactly when deleting a $\mu$-null set from $X$ never lower its Hausdorff dimension. I.e., when the mass of $\mu$ on $X$ is spread across all of the dimension of $X$. The following proposition gives two sufficient conditions for this:

:::{prf:proposition} Implicit in the proof of Theorem 13 of {cite:t}`Stu21`
:label: proposition:full_upper_dimension
Let $X \subseteq \Omega$ with $s := \dim_{\operatorname{H}}(X)$, and let $\mu$ be an outer measure on $\Omega$ with $\mu(X) > 0$. Then $\overline{\dim}_{\operatorname{H}}(\mu \llcorner X) = s$ whenever either:
1. $\mu \ll \mathcal{H}^{s - \delta}$ for every $0 < \delta \leq s$, (in which case, $\underline{\dim}_{\operatorname{H}}(\mu \llcorner X) = s$, too); or
2. $\mathcal{H}^s \ll \mu$ and $\mathcal{H}^s(X) > 0$.
:::

:::{prf:proof}
We already have that $\overline{\dim}_{\operatorname{H}}(\mu \llcorner X) \leq s$. Now we prove the other direction for each condition. 

1. If $\mu(E \cap X) > 0$ for some $E \subseteq \Omega$, then $E \cap X$ is not $(s - \delta)$-null, so $\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(E) \geq s - \delta$ for every $0 < \delta \leq s$. Hence $s \leq \underline{\dim}_{\operatorname{H}}(\mu \llcorner X) \leq \overline{\dim}_{\operatorname{H}}(\mu \llcorner X)$. 
2. If $E$ is (\mu \llcorner X)-conull, i.e., $\mu(X \setminus E) = 0$, then $\mathcal{H}^s(X \setminus E) = 0$, so $0 < \mathcal{H}^s(X) \leq \mathcal{H}^s(X \cap E)$ and $\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(E) \geq s$. So $$\overline{\dim}_{\operatorname{H}}(\mu \llcorner X) \geq s$, too.
:::

{cite:t}`Stu21` assumes these two conditions in his sufficient condition for the existence of optimal Hausdorff oracles.

Note that while the first condition actually proves equality for lower Hausdorff dimension as well, the second condition does not control the lower dimension. To be concrete, suppose $s > 0$, that $\mathcal{H}^s(X) > 0$, and $x \in X$. Then $\mu := \mathcal{H}^s + \delta_x$ (where $\delta_x$ is the point mass at $x$) satisfies the second condition while $\underline{\dim}_{\operatorname{H}}(\mu \llcorner X) = 0$.
